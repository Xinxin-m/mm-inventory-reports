Exp8 — 10 roster MM2026-09-04

Descriptive, not causal. MMs appear by volume rank only.

Hyperliquid · HYPE perpetual · 2025-03-27 to 2026-06-30 UTC

Market-maker inventory events on HYPE

We study 922 one-sided taker surges on HYPE and the market makers (MMs) around them. A surge is a half hour in which taker buys, or taker sells, jump far above their level over the previous week and far outweigh the other side. Each surge is timed by its run: the minutes in which the surging side keeps outtrading the other (median 14 minutes). Around each surge we follow every active HYPE MM, a median 100 per surge. Two short pages carry the results and a third defines which MMs count as active. A fourth page asks which MMs lose money, on the older net-flow events.

Key results

A surge shocks six times as many MMs as the same clock time on a nearby day without a surge
An MM is shocked when its HYPE position changes during the surge by at least 3 times its own typical 24-hour swing. 5.9% of active MMs are shocked in a surge, against 1.0% in control windows: the same clock time and length on a nearby day with no surge. 90% of the shocked MMs moved the way a maker filling the takers moves: they bought when the takers sold, and sold when the takers bought.
One-sided taker surges, Section 2 →
The spread nearly doubles at the flow peak and is close to normal about an hour later
Around the minute with the most surge taker volume, the bid-ask spread is 1.82 times its mean over the event's own 12-hour baseline. An hour after the run it is 6% above that mean; after a liquidation cascade it is still 35% above. The resting size within 10 bps of the mid stays at its level before the surge, so takers pay through the wider spread rather than a thinner book.
What a surge does to the book, Section 1 →
Trades, not cancels, move the best quote away, and it does not come back during the surge
The surge-side best quote is the best price on the side the takers hit (the best ask in a buy surge). On dates where every book update is stored, trades cause 57% to 63% of its move away from the mid during a surge, against 32% to 35% in quiet control windows; cancellations cause most of the rest. After a trade moves the quote away, the quote moves about as far again away from the mid over the next 50 updates that move that best price or trade at it.
What a surge does to the book, Sections 2 and 3 →
Whether the price comes back goes with the taker flow after the run, not with the MMs
Sort the surges into five equal groups by how far the price comes back in the two hours after the run. In every group the MMs absorb the same 27% to 36% of the surge and quote the same way. In one regression of the two-hour reversal on eight candidate measures, only the net taker flow in the 15 minutes after the run has a coefficient distinguishable from zero; the two windows overlap, so this is an association. At the end of the run the MMs hold a position equal to 26% to 32% of the surge's taker volume in every group. Two hours later they hold none of it where the price came back most, and 23% where the price kept going.
One-sided taker surges, Section 3 →
MMs lean toward the exit by about one basis point at most
The absorbing side is the side of the book the surging takers hit; the exit side is the other side, where an MM can unload what it absorbed. Compared with its own 2 hours before the surge, an MM places new absorbing-side orders further from the best quote than new exit-side orders. The gap is 0.6 bps while the MM builds its position and 1.1 bps in the first 5 minutes after its position peaks. Most of the gap comes from the absorbing-side orders moving back.
